Case Scenario: Northstar Campus Portal

Northstar College is preparing to release a campus portal used for enrollment, grade viewing, and fee assessment. The QA team reports the following information:

  • Module A: 24 confirmed defects in 12 KLOC. | Module B: 18 confirmed defects in 6 KLOC.
  • Defect Removal: Before release, the team detected and removed 90 defects. After release, users reported 10 additional defects.
  • Test Coverage: 90 of 100 requirements were tested. However, the untested requirements include a payment-authorization workflow.
  • Ticket Trends: The defect tracker shows fewer open tickets this month, but the number of reopened tickets and production incidents has increased.
  • Governance & Audit: The release procedure requires an approved test report and an authorized change request before deployment. A sampled production release has a deployment record but no approval record.
  • Reviews & Documentation: Developers say they always follow the review process, but repository history has not been audited. Test reports are frequently incomplete due to an outdated procedure and unconstrained template. Management wants to close the finding immediately after reminding developers to be more careful.

Part I — Quantitative Analysis and Interpretation (25 Points)

1. Calculate the defect density of Module A and Module B. Show the formula, substitution, units, and final values. (8 points)
Formula: Defect Density = (Total Confirmed Defects) ÷ (Module Size in KLOC)
• Module A: 24 defects ÷ 12 KLOC = 2.0 defects/KLOC
• Module B: 18 defects ÷ 6 KLOC = 3.0 defects/KLOC
Final Values with Units: Module A has a defect density of 2.0 defects/KLOC. Module B has a defect density of 3.0 defects/KLOC.
2. Compare the two modules. Explain why raw defect counts alone would lead to a misleading conclusion. (5 points)
Comparison & Analysis: Comparing raw defect counts alone (24 in Module A vs. 18 in Module B) falsely suggests that Module A has poorer code quality simply because 24 > 18. This conclusion is misleading because it ignores module size and code volume. Module A is twice the size of Module B (12 KLOC vs. 6 KLOC). Normalizing defects against size reveals that Module B has a defect density of 3.0 defects/KLOC, which is 50% higher than Module A's 2.0 defects/KLOC. This proves Module B is significantly more defect-prone per unit of code, indicating higher internal structural complexity, less thorough peer reviews, or weaker construction practices. Raw defect counts measure only gross defect volume, whereas defect density reflects actual defect concentration and relative code quality.
3. Calculate Defect Removal Efficiency (DRE) using the scenario data and the formula DRE = E ÷ (E + D) × 100. Interpret what the result does and does not prove. (7 points)
Formula: DRE = [E ÷ (E + D)] × 100
Variables: E = 90 (pre-release defects detected and removed); D = 10 (post-release defects reported by users)
DRE = [90 ÷ (90 + 10)] × 100 = (90 ÷ 100) × 100 = 90.0%
Interpretation:
  • What it PROVES: It proves that the pre-release quality filtering, testing, and review activities successfully caught and eliminated 90.0% of the total defects that became visible within the observed evaluation window (90 out of 100 total detected defects).
  • What it DOES NOT PROVE: (1) It does not prove the system is high quality or defect-free, as it only measures known, discovered defects; dormant defects may still reside in production. (2) It does not reflect defect severity or business impact; if the 10 post-release defects involve catastrophic payment failures or grade corruption, the system suffers severe disruption despite an ostensibly high 90% score. (3) It does not prove comprehensive test coverage, particularly since 10 requirements (including core payment authorization) were never tested.
4. The team reports 90% requirements coverage. Explain why this percentage is not enough to justify release approval in this scenario. Identify the next evidence or test action you would prioritize. (5 points)
Explanation: Requirements coverage (90/100 = 90%) is an unweighted quantitative metric that treats all functional requirements as having equal operational, financial, and security risk. In this scenario, the unverified 10% contains the payment-authorization workflow—a mission-critical financial transaction path. Releasing an untested payment workflow exposes Northstar College to severe risks: financial discrepancies, unauthorized student charges, regulatory non-compliance, security vulnerabilities, and reputational damage. High aggregate coverage can never compensate for zero coverage of high-impact transaction features. Furthermore, the defect tracker's rising trend of reopened tickets and production incidents indicates active architectural instability.

Next Prioritized Action & Evidence: Immediately halt release authorization and prioritize rigorous end-to-end functional, negative, and security integration testing of the payment-authorization workflow (gateway handshakes, token validation, edge-case timeouts, concurrent charges, and rollback integrity), substantiated by an approved Requirements Traceability Matrix (RTM) and signed test execution logs.

Part II — Audit Judgment and Evidence (25 Points)

5. Distinguish the testing, inspection, and audit activities that would be relevant to the missing payment-authorization tests, code review, and absent release approval. Give one scenario-specific example of each. (8 points)
Distinction of SQA Activities with Scenario-Specific Examples:
  • Testing (Dynamic Quality Control): The dynamic execution of compiled software code with selected test inputs on a runtime environment to validate behavior against specifications and uncover runtime defects.
    Scenario Example: Executing dynamic test scripts against the payment-authorization workflow with valid, invalid, and expired payment tokens to verify that tuition charges process accurately, decline improper requests, and handle network timeouts without state loss.
  • Inspection (Static Peer Review): A formal, systematic engineering examination of static work products (source code, architecture models, requirements) conducted line-by-line by peers without code execution to detect defects, standards violations, and logic flaws early.
    Scenario Example: Conducting a formal code inspection of Module B's pull requests (reviewing its 6 KLOC) using a standardized inspection checklist to examine error-handling routines, database calls, and adherence to security coding rules, recorded in a formal review log.
  • Audit (Independent Quality Assurance & Compliance): An independent, evidence-based management evaluation conducted by authorized auditors to determine whether operational activities, processes, and records comply with documented institutional policies, procedures, and regulatory standards.
    Scenario Example: An SQA auditor evaluating production deployment records to verify whether the mandatory pre-release change authorization and signed QA test reports were archived prior to deploying the campus portal release.
6. State the applicable audit criterion in the missing-approval case and identify the objective evidence needed to support a defensible finding. Do not rely on assumptions or interview statements alone. (7 points)
Applicable Audit Criterion: Northstar College Release Procedure / Policy Clause: "The release procedure requires an approved test report and an authorized change request before deployment."

Objective Evidence Needed for a Defensible Finding:
  1. The Production Deployment Record / Server Log showing the exact build ID, deployment timestamp, and deployer identity for the sampled release.
  2. The Change Management Repository / Ticket Log corresponding to that deployment, demonstrating the complete absence of an approved Change Request (CR) or authorization sign-off prior to the deployment timestamp.
  3. The QA Documentation Archive confirming that no approved final test summary report exists for the deployed build.
Audit Justification: Cross-referencing the deployment timestamp against the missing pre-deployment approval records provides indisputable, verifiable documentary evidence without relying on developer interview statements.
7. Explain whether the current evidence is sufficient to conclude that every production release is nonconforming. Describe how sampling and scope should affect the conclusion. (5 points)
Evaluation: No, the current evidence is not sufficient to conclude that every production release is nonconforming. The audit team examined only a single sampled production release.

Role of Sampling and Scope: In SQA auditing (aligned with ISO 19011 standards), a single audit sample proves nonconformity only for that specific instance. Drawing a universal conclusion ("every release is nonconforming") based on one sample is an unsupported generalization that compromises audit credibility. To determine whether this gap represents an isolated human oversight or a systemic procedural breakdown, the auditor must expand the sample size by auditing a statistically valid sample (e.g., 10 to 20 recent production releases over the past 6 to 12 months). The audit finding and scope must remain strictly proportional to the verified sample population.
8. Identify two additional sources of evidence that could corroborate or challenge the developers’ claim that reviews are always performed. Explain what each source could establish. (5 points)
Two Corroborating Sources of Evidence:
  • 1. Git Version Control / Pull Request (PR) History & Branch Protection Logs (GitHub / GitLab):
    What it Establishes: Provides immutable, timestamped digital records of code merges, peer review approvals, inline discussion comments, and author vs. reviewer identities. This directly establishes whether peer reviews actually took place prior to merging code, whether reviews were substantive or merely rubber-stamped, or whether code was committed directly to main/production branches bypassing review requirements.
  • 2. Formal Peer Review Meeting Records, Checklists, and Inspection Defect Logs:
    What it Establishes: Demonstrates whether formal inspection meetings were scheduled, who attended, time spent conducting reviews, what defects were identified, and whether identified defects were tracked to verified resolution before release.

Part III — Root Cause and Corrective Action Plan (25 Points)

9. Write a concise audit finding for the release with missing approval. Include the requirement, verified evidence, and the gap between them. (6 points)
Statement of Audit Finding (Nonconformity):
  • Requirement (Audit Criterion): According to the Northstar College Release Procedure, every production deployment requires an approved test report and an authorized change request prior to release execution.
  • Verified Evidence (Condition): An audit of production deployment records for Release [Sampled ID] confirmed deployment to the production campus portal environment on [Date/Timestamp]. However, examination of the change management system and QA repository revealed that no authorized change request exists, and no approved test summary report was signed off or archived for this release.
  • Gap (Nonconformity): The production deployment was executed without the required authorized change request and approved test report, violating release governance procedures and circumventing deployment quality gates.
10. Use a structured root-cause method (such as the 5 Whys or a cause-and-effect analysis) to investigate why test reports are incomplete. Show at least three connected reasoning steps and distinguish a symptom from a likely root cause. (7 points)
Structured Root-Cause Analysis (5 Whys Method):
Observed Symptom: Earlier test reports are frequently incomplete.
  1. Why are earlier test reports frequently incomplete?
    → Because QA testers routinely omit critical testing metrics, coverage summaries, and sign-off fields.
  2. Why do testers omit these critical sections?
    → Because the test report template contains no mandatory fields, input validation, or clear guidance requiring them.
  3. Why does the template lack mandatory fields and guidance?
    → Because the test documentation procedure has not been reviewed, standardized, or updated alongside portal development.
  4. Why has the documentation procedure not been updated and enforced?
    → Because there is no assigned SQA process ownership or formal document governance mechanism to audit and maintain SQA procedural standards.
Distinction: The symptom is testers submitting incomplete documentation with missing data (an observable operational behavior). The root cause is the absence of an updated, standardized documentation procedure and an unconstrained, non-enforcing template that permits incomplete deliverables to pass without gatekeeping.
11. Propose a corrective action plan that addresses the root cause rather than only reminding staff to be careful. Include the action, responsible role, evidence of implementation, and how effectiveness will be checked. (8 points)
Corrective Action Plan:
  • Action: (a) Revise and re-issue the Test Documentation and Release Governance Procedure to mandate standardized reporting sections (test coverage, defect metrics, sign-off blocks). (b) Replace the unstructured test report template with an electronic template in the QA management tool (e.g., Jira/TestRail) that enforces mandatory fields and blocks submission if required data is absent. (c) Implement automated CI/CD deployment pipeline hard-gates that verify digital attachments of an approved test report and authorized change request before permitting deployment scripts to execute.
  • Responsible Role: SQA Lead / QA Manager (procedural & template updates) and DevOps / Release Engineering Lead (automated CI/CD deployment gate configuration).
  • Evidence of Implementation: (1) Published, approved, and version-controlled SQA Test Documentation Procedure v2.0; (2) Deployed electronic test report template with enforced mandatory fields; (3) Staff training attendance and acknowledgment records; (4) CI/CD pipeline configuration scripts enforcing approval verification gates before release promotion.
  • Checking Effectiveness: SQA Lead and Internal Audit will conduct bi-weekly audits of 100% of generated test reports and production deployments over the next quarter (3 months) to verify zero incomplete test reports and zero unauthorized deployments.
12. State what evidence must be reviewed during a follow-up audit before the significant finding can be closed. (4 points)
Required Evidence for Audit Finding Closure:
Before the significant nonconformity finding can be closed, the follow-up audit must examine:
  1. A sample of recent production deployment records (e.g., the last 5 consecutive deployments executed post-corrective action), verifying that each deployment has a corresponding authorized change request with a verified prior timestamp.
  2. The corresponding approved, complete test reports for those releases, verifying that all mandatory fields, coverage metrics, and sign-offs are completed.
  3. Active CI/CD pipeline deployment logs demonstrating that automated release gates successfully block deployments when approvals are absent.
Audit Note: Management's proposal to close the finding after verbally reminding developers to be more careful must be explicitly rejected; verbal reminders do not constitute verified corrective action or objective evidence.
